In the [post about moving back to Bratislava](/posts/2026-08-28-from-spain-to-bratislava-again/) I wrote that when Klassify started, "the circle closes again, for the second time". That sentence deserves more than a half-line, because the circle is the shape of my whole working life: one-man company, a company of friends, a big company, and a one-man company again.

## Loop one: on my own

I kinda did the very first "mini loop" in Bratislava, in the 90s, before the emigration. We started a company with friends, and I also had self-employed status for some time, as a side thing in addition to working in multiple places. Those were crazy times, and one had to have more than one job to survive the rapid change that Czechoslovakia, and later Slovakia, went through after the regime change in 1989. When we decided to emigrate (which we did in 1998), this whole chapter closed.

In the U.S.A. I was on an H1B visa, so I worked for somebody else, and after landing in Canada, starting as a freelancer didn't look like a great idea to me. New country, new language, new society, no experience, and building a business in that environment: I didn't feel that would be the easiest start. Fortunately, I found Montage, an Ottawa software company, and got employed. Those were the pre-2000 years, and software engineers were sought after. Here I met Peter and Steve, two very important people for my next phase, and many other dear friends with whom I'm still in contact today. I stayed there until the Montage acquisition by AT&T Canada in 2003, became a lucky victim of the corporate restructuring, got a package, and went freelancing.

"Miro Adamy Software Solutions" was a one-man company in Ottawa, from mid-2003 until Thinknostic. In practice it was a contracting vehicle: I went where the interesting project was, as a tech lead on a government portal in Ottawa or as a consultant for a chip manufacturer. I even tested my first remote project way before it was standard: a friend of mine started a company in Switzerland, and I was working for them remotely, over a 6-hour time difference. Me being a night owl and him being an early riser created an interesting sync-up window. Between midnight and 1:00 a.m. in Canada, which was 6 to 7 a.m. in Switzerland, we synced up at the end of my working day and before the start of his. Of course we also communicated during the day, in his late afternoon, after I woke up, but this was my first experience with time zones and remote work.

I have fond recollections of this time. The disadvantage was that it was kinda lonely. And there was absolutely no work-personal boundary. I was mentally always at work. The problem was that work topics and personal topics happened mostly in the same room, behind the same computer. I remember declaring two pairs of slippers, dedicated work slippers and home slippers, and changing them at the end of the work hours when I switched from work topics to personal topics.

So in early 2004 I started to talk to a couple of friends from Montage about how great it would be to work together, to do something together, and to have a company that would be different. One that would create a good place for people like us, to come and enjoy working among their own tribe. We even had a term for that tribe, which we used when we were looking out for like-minded people to join us: "sociable geeks".

## Loop two: a company of friends

In 2004 I co-founded Thinknostic, in Ottawa. It was a small startup that did what small startups do: everybody did everything.
We were lucky in that at the beginning we landed a few important clients, which gave us references, and that made the company survive the critical period where 8 out of 10 startups fail. The original group of founders were all techies. But after a couple of months, we managed to convince the former Montage CEO to join us and lead the small company from the business point of view.

Thinknostic grew and renamed itself Thinkwrap as part of a merger/acquisition and the expansion to Toronto. In 2008 we started to focus on eCommerce and became a company with a name in the Canadian market, specializing in Oracle Commerce (ATG, Endeca) and later Hybris (today SAP Commerce) implementations for retailers in Canada and the US. My own role kept shifting with it - team lead, architect, trainer and mentor for new people, and more and more the person who kept the systems running. Looking back, that was the moment the DevOps part of my career started, before I had a name for it.

In 2015 the company opened an office in Valencia, and the next year I moved there to build the operations side, so that releases for North American clients could happen in the European morning. Part 2 tells what that did to our family life; professionally, it gave me the small-growing-company energy a second time.

## Loop three: a big company

In 2018 Thinkwrap merged with Tenzing and Spark::red, and the combined company was renamed Pivotree in 2019. Overnight the company of friends was part of something much larger. We more than tripled in size. Our HQ was in Toronto. We had data centers, which were already on the way out, moving to the cloud. We suddenly had offices on the West and East Coast, in Texas, and also in India, in addition to Ottawa, Toronto and Valencia. Things started to change (as they do in a big company) and the acquisitions continued: over the years 2018 to 2024 several more companies were acquired by Pivotree, the focus shifting more and more toward India. It changed the spirit, dynamism and passion of the small company a lot, and not necessarily for the better.

I moved fully into DevOps and cloud: AWS, automation, Kubernetes, the platform that other teams build on. As a result of the acquisition, we had hundreds of clients in AWS whose accounts we actively maintained and worked with. It is a very different story to manage 2 or 3 AWS accounts than to manage 100+ of them. It calls for completely different tooling and workflows, and a completely different level of paranoia when it comes to security..

The online transformation and the switch to remote work, driven by COVID, was quick and efficient. From March 2020 I worked fully remote from Bratislava, and for four years it did not matter much where my desk was. Remote work brought back the same challenges I had seen in 2003 and 2004, before starting Thinknostic. I rarely met a coworker, even during my occasional visits to Valencia, as a big part of the team in Spain was already gone, and most of my friends from Canada had also left the company. So I started to ask myself "what am I still doing here".

## Back to one

Luckily, a corporate restructuring ended my employment in May 2024 and made that decision for me. In the same month Klassify s.r.o. entered the Slovak company register. A one-man company again - the third time, if I count Bratislava in the '90s.

It is the same shape as twenty years ago, but not the same thing: twenty years ago a one-man company meant selling hours, today it means choosing problems. And on top of that, with AI tools one person can cover ground that needed a small team in 2004.

What I do with that freedom, and what I say no to, is the subject of the last part.
