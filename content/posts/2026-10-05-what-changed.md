In the [first post of this series](/posts/2026-08-22-the-five-year-intermission/) I wrote, in one line, that I changed my priorities and my focus in many areas of life quite significantly. Eleven parts later, it is time to say what that line meant.

## What changed

Most of you reading this know me, so I can skip the polite version. The biggest change is simple: I don't optimize for money anymore. I optimize for happiness. Not because there is too much money, but because at some point "how much does it pay" stopped being the first question, and I noticed it had been the first question for twenty years.

What replaced it is a sliding scale I never designed. I just caught myself using it. How flexible I am about pay depends entirely on how much I love the thing.

At one end is Wikipedia. Nobody pays me to edit it. The opposite is true: I travel for it and I spend my own money on it, and I come home happy. The same goes for an open-source project, if it is a good one, it interests me, and the people in it are the kind I would want to have a beer with. That I would do for free, and I would be the one saying thank you.

At the other end is your business problem. It is a real problem, it needs solving, and it has a price. A fair one, but a price. The passion discount does not apply, because the passion is not there. The craft is.

And then there is the far end, the one with the warning sign. If the job means me working on Windows, or in one of the technologies I despise, or being on call, or another person or company deciding how I allocate my time, the price is freaking high. Expensive-lawyer high. The kind of number that makes no sense to the client, and that is the point. I am hoping the number says no for me, so that I do not have to. It is a filter, and I hope it does its job.

I know how this sounds: a guy with a one-man s.r.o. and a blog declaring he is above money. I am not above it. I just let the project decide what it is worth to me, and the "unit of worthiness" is not euros. I simply stopped asking what a project pays before asking whether I want it. It must be something that I love and want to do, and that is the gate. Nobody pays me to read, to hike, or to learn anyway.

## How I work now

All of this shows up in how I choose work, and the short version is: I am picky, on purpose.

I do not take permanent employment, and I do not work fully on-site. I may take part-time projects, two or three days a week max, lasting from two months up to about a year. I prefer clients in Europe, the UK, Canada and the US East Coast, ideally remote, or hybrid around Bratislava or Vienna, and provided that they appeal to me from a technology point of view and from a business vertical point of view.

If that sounds like a match for your requirements, the details are on [my consulting page](/consulting/).

## And this blog

This is the last part of the catching-up series. The blog does not stop with it: the next posts are the ones that did not fit here, starting with how my health data flows from a watch into my notes without me lifting a finger, and how an AI helped bring this twenty-year-old blog back to life.

One final thought. Looking back at the technology changes I was part of, they remind me of a pendulum, swinging between two extremes: everything on the server, and everything on the client.

When I started, everything was on the server: mainframes, minicomputers, computing centers. Then came the rise of the PCs, and suddenly everybody had a computer of their own. Desktop applications exploded: everybody ran everything on their own PC and used the network only to share and exchange data. Then the PCs got networked for real, server-side applications appeared, and the weight moved back to the server. The internet took over, the web made applications truly distributed, and the public cloud providers built data centers the size of towns, the server in its purest form: the server became a building. Then web technologies and JavaScript grew up and brought everything back to the client, and the mobile phone put immense computing power, and an app market, into everyone's pocket. Today the big language models are far too heavy to run anywhere but on a server, and yet the small local models are getting good enough to be useful.

I feel lucky and privileged to have lived through the years that transformed technology more than anything before. I think this pendulum will never stop, and I'm looking forward to seeing it swing a few more times in the coming years.
